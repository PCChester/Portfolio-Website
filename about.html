The origin

Thirty years of crossing borders

I moved to Austria in 1995 as a stranger in a strange land — no shared language, no familiar cultural shorthand. Within a year, I was teaching others how to navigate that exact experience. What started as English as a Second Language classes became corporate communication training, and a career built entirely around one question: how do you help someone say what they mean, and be understood?

Why this matters now

Learning AI is like learning a language

I've spent three decades helping people move through the same learning curve — whether the subject was English grammar, a pressure gauge 20 metres underwater, or a boardroom presentation in a second language. The progression is always the same.

Stage 1
Vocabulary
What is this tool? What do these terms mean? Why does it sometimes give me nonsense?
Stage 2
Grammar
How do I structure a prompt to get useful output? What does context actually change?
Stage 3
Fluency
The tool stops feeling like a chore and becomes how you work. You stop noticing you're using it.

When a team member says "I don't understand why it gave me that answer," most technical consultants see a prompt error. I see a language acquisition problem — and I know exactly which stage that person is on, and what they need next.

"I spent 30 years teaching humans to communicate with each other. Now I teach humans and machines to work together."
